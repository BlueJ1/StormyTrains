Project in Data Science · corridor comparison

Three corridors → München

Berlin Hbf, Hamburg Hbf and Frankfurt (Main) Hbf to München Hbf: every ICE and IC run that stopped at the origin and later at München, 1 July 2024 to 31 August 2026. The same rules and severity classes on all three, railway data only, no weather.

Outcomes

Similar shares of bad journeys, different kinds of lateness

The severe-or-canceled share is close on all three corridors: 12.8% from Berlin, 14.9% from Hamburg and 13.8% from Frankfurt. Frankfurt, the shortest trip, has the fewest punctual arrivals (38.9%) and a median delay of 10 min: more trains are moderately late, but no more are severely late than elsewhere. Hamburg has the most cancellations (8.0%).

Routes and length

Frankfurt is short and simple; Hamburg is long and varied

From Frankfurt, 72 stop patterns fall into two main routes of similar length. From Hamburg there are 508 patterns, and the slowest 10% of runs take 9 h 06 or more. Berlin sits in between, with one dominant high-speed route and a long tail of detours.

Over time

Bad months are largely shared

The monthly severe-or-canceled shares move together: the correlation is 0.84 between Berlin and Hamburg, 0.72 between Hamburg and Frankfurt, and 0.66 between Berlin and Frankfurt. July 2025 and January 2026 are bad on all three. Part of this is shared trains, part is whatever affects the whole network at once.

During the journey

Frankfurt trains bring their delay with them

50% of trains leave Frankfurt at least 6 minutes late, against 26% at Berlin and 24% at Hamburg, because most Frankfurt runs started further back. So the delay at departure already says a lot about the outcome there (correlation 0.65), and little at Hamburg (0.26). On all three corridors the correlation climbs to about 0.9 once three quarters of the schedule is done.

Cancellations

Hamburg loses the most journeys

Most canceled journeys are canceled at both ends. Hamburg has a larger share canceled only at the origin, where the train then ran later in the journey, and the most journeys that lost only intermediate stops.

Shared trains

The same run can count on more than one corridor

A train from Hamburg via Berlin to München is a Hamburg journey and a Berlin journey. 8,706 runs are in both of those corridors, a third of Hamburg's. 4,991 Berlin runs go via Frankfurt and are Frankfurt journeys too. One ICE 699 run is even in all three. Their outcomes at München are identical, so any split into training and test data has to keep a run on one side.

Data quality

Frankfurt's stop data is the most complete

Before the source widened its station coverage on 3 November 2025, most Berlin and Hamburg journeys have gaps in their stop numbering; far fewer Frankfurt journeys do. After the change, gaps are rare everywhere.

For the project

Choosing the study pairs

The project instructions ask us to compare two or three candidate origin–destination pairs and pick one or two. These are the trade-offs visible in the railway data. The choice is ours to make together.

One corridor or two?

Berlin and Hamburg share a third of Hamburg's runs and most of their bad months, so as a pair they add less independent data. Frankfurt shares fewer runs and behaves differently at departure.

Does trip length matter for the question?

Frankfurt (3 h 22) gives a short, simple corridor; Hamburg (6 h 37) a long one with many routes. Weather along a 6-hour trip is harder to summarise than along a 3-hour one.

What is known at prediction time?

For Frankfurt, the delay the train brings in is the strongest single signal. Whether it counts as known before departure depends on the prediction cutoff we choose.

How much data is enough?

All three have 22,544 or more journeys and about 3,100 or more positives, so any of them supports the core models.
